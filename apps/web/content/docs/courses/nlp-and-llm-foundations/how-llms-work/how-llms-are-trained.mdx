## Overview

A model like Claude isn't trained once, with one objective — it goes
through several distinct stages, each with a different goal, each building
on the previous one. Understanding these stages explains a lot about *why*
LLMs behave the way they do: why they sometimes sound like they're
predicting text rather than conversing, why instruction-following had to be
taught separately, and why "alignment" is a real, separate engineering
problem from raw capability.

## Stage 1: Pretraining — next-token prediction at massive scale

The base model is trained on one deceptively simple objective across a
huge corpus of text (a large fraction of the internet, books, code):
predict the next token, given everything before it. That's it — no
labels, no human curation of "correct answers," just: given this sequence
so far, what token comes next?

This objective, at sufficient scale, turns out to force the model to learn
an enormous amount implicitly — grammar, facts, reasoning patterns, coding
conventions — because accurately predicting the next token in, say, a
worked math proof or a Python function *requires* something functionally
like understanding the content, not just surface-level pattern matching.
The result of this stage is a **base model**: extremely knowledgeable, but
prone to just continuing text in whatever style it started, not
necessarily "answering a question" the way a human assistant would.

## Stage 2: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) — teaching the assistant format

A base model completes text; it doesn't inherently know it's supposed to
behave like a helpful assistant responding to a user. **SFT** fixes this by
further training the model on a (much smaller, carefully curated) dataset
of example conversations: a prompt, paired with a high-quality example of
how a helpful assistant should respond. This teaches the model the
*format* and *behavior* of being an assistant — following instructions,
staying on topic, refusing harmful requests — on top of the knowledge
pretraining already gave it.

## Stage 3: RLHF — refining behavior from human preferences

**RLHF** (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) goes a step further:
rather than training on fixed example responses, it trains on *comparisons*
— humans (or a model trained to imitate human judgment) rank multiple
candidate responses to the same prompt from best to worst, and the model is
optimized to produce responses more like the ones ranked highly. This stage
is what most directly shapes qualities that are hard to specify with fixed
example responses: being appropriately cautious, admitting uncertainty
instead of confidently making things up, being genuinely helpful rather
than just superficially compliant.

## Why this matters for how you use these models

- **Hallucination** traces partly back to Stage 1 — a next-token predictor
  trained to always continue plausibly will produce a plausible-sounding
  answer even when it doesn't actually know one, unless later stages have
  specifically trained it to express uncertainty instead.
- **Following unusual instruction formats well** is a Stage 2/3 skill, not
  guaranteed by raw capability — a very knowledgeable base model can still
  be bad at reliably following a specific output format without adequate
  fine-tuning on exactly that kind of task.
- **"Jailbreaks"** (covered in the Applied/Production course's ethics
  material) often specifically target the gap between what Stage 1
  knowledge makes the model *capable* of producing and what Stages 2-3
  trained it to actually produce by default.
