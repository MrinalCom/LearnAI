## Overview

Every model on this platform, from Classical ML's logistic regression
onward, learns patterns from training data — including whatever biases
exist in that data. This isn't a niche edge case: a hiring model trained on
historical hiring decisions learns whatever bias existed in *those*
decisions; a language model trained on internet text learns whatever
skewed representations exist across the internet. Understanding where this
comes from and how to measure it is a practical engineering responsibility,
not just an abstract ethical concern.

## Where bias actually enters a system

- **Historical bias** — training data reflects real historical patterns
  that were themselves unfair (e.g., a hiring dataset reflecting decades
  of biased hiring decisions faithfully teaches a model to make similarly
  biased decisions).
- **Representation bias** — some groups are underrepresented in training
  data relative to the population the model will actually serve, so the
  model has proportionally less signal to learn accurate patterns for
  them.
- **Measurement bias** — the labels themselves can encode bias (e.g., an
  imperfect proxy for the real target, applied inconsistently across
  groups).

None of these require anyone to have intentionally built a biased system —
they emerge from data reflecting the world (and its existing inequities)
as it actually is, which is exactly why deliberate measurement and
mitigation are necessary rather than optional.

## Measuring fairness: there's no single definition

Multiple, mathematically incompatible definitions of "fair" exist, which is
part of why this is hard rather than a simple checkbox:

- **Demographic parity** — the model's positive prediction rate should be
  equal across groups.
- **Equalized odds** — the model's true positive rate and false positive
  rate should be equal across groups (connects directly to the Classical
  ML course's precision/recall metrics, evaluated separately per group
  rather than in aggregate).
- **Individual fairness** — similar individuals should receive similar
  predictions, regardless of group membership.

It's mathematically proven that you generally *cannot* satisfy all of these
simultaneously except in special cases — choosing which fairness
definition matters most is a genuine judgment call specific to the
application, not something a formula settles for you.

## Practical mitigation

- **Audit training data composition** before training, not after
  deployment — check representation across the groups that matter for your
  application.
- **Evaluate metrics per subgroup**, not just in aggregate — a model with
  strong overall accuracy can still have a large, hidden accuracy gap for
  a specific subgroup that aggregate numbers completely mask.
- **Involve people with domain and ethics expertise** in defining what
  fairness means for your specific application — this isn't a purely
  technical decision a model architecture choice can resolve on its own.
