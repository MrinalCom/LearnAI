## Overview

L1/L2 regularization from the Classical ML course still applies to neural
networks — penalizing weight magnitude works the same way regardless of how
many layers there are. But deep networks have millions of parameters and
overfit easily, so a few techniques specific to deep learning have become
standard on top of L1/L2.

## Dropout: randomly delete neurons during training

**Dropout** randomly "turns off" a fraction of neurons (commonly 20-50%) on
each training step — their output is forced to zero, and they don't
participate in that step's forward or backward pass at all.

```
during training: each neuron has probability p of being zeroed out
during inference: all neurons active, outputs scaled to compensate
```

Why this helps: it prevents neurons from **co-adapting** — relying on very
specific combinations of other neurons to correct their mistakes. Since any
neuron might be dropped at any step, the network is forced to learn
features that are useful somewhat independently, which is a more robust,
less overfit representation. It's a similar spirit to a random forest's
bagging (from Classical ML) — introducing randomness during training to
prevent the model from overfitting to spurious detail.

## Batch normalization: keep layer inputs well-behaved

As training proceeds, the *distribution* of values flowing into each layer
keeps shifting as earlier layers' weights update — a problem called
**internal covariate shift** that makes deeper networks slower and more
finicky to train. **Batch normalization** re-centers and re-scales each
layer's inputs (using the mean/variance of the current mini-batch) before
they hit the next layer, keeping that distribution stable throughout
training. In practice this lets you use higher learning rates and makes
the network noticeably less sensitive to how weights were initialized —
it's as much a training-speed technique as a regularization one, though it
does have a regularizing side effect from the noise introduced by
per-batch statistics.

## Early stopping: just stop before you overfit

The simplest technique of all: track validation loss (using the
cross-validation ideas from Classical ML) during training, and stop as soon
as it starts getting *worse* even while training loss keeps improving —
that gap is the model starting to memorize the training set instead of
generalizing.

```
epoch 10: train_loss=0.42, val_loss=0.45
epoch 20: train_loss=0.31, val_loss=0.38
epoch 30: train_loss=0.22, val_loss=0.39   <- val loss went up, stop here
epoch 40: train_loss=0.15, val_loss=0.44   <- would have kept getting worse
```

## Putting it together

A typical modern network combines several of these at once: L2
regularization on the weights, dropout between fully-connected layers,
batch normalization after convolutional/linear layers, and early stopping
to decide when training is done — none of these compete with each other,
they address overfitting and unstable training from different angles
simultaneously.
