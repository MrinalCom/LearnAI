## Overview

Machine learning is fundamentally about making predictions under
uncertainty, and probability is the mathematical language for describing
that uncertainty precisely. A handful of concepts from this lesson show up
by name throughout the rest of this platform.

## Mean and variance: the two numbers that summarize a distribution

- **Mean** — the average value: `Σx / n`. The single-number "center" of
  a set of data.
- **Variance** — how spread out the data is around that mean:
  `Σ(x − mean)² / n`. Low variance means values cluster tightly around
  the mean; high variance means they're spread widely.
- **Standard deviation** — the square root of variance, back in the same
  units as the original data (variance is in *squared* units, which is
  hard to interpret directly).

These two numbers reappear constantly: feature scaling (Classical ML's
KNN lesson) standardizes using exactly mean and standard deviation, and
the "variance" in the bias-variance tradeoff is a direct application of
this same concept to a model's *predictions* rather than raw data.

## Probability distributions

A probability distribution describes how likely different outcomes are.
Two you'll see referenced throughout this platform:

- **Normal (Gaussian) distribution** — the familiar bell curve, where
  values cluster around the mean and become rarer the further out you go.
  Many real-world quantities (heights, measurement errors) approximate
  this shape, which is why it's the default choice for generating
  synthetic data in this platform's own playgrounds (`rng.normal(...)`
  appears in dozens of them).
- **Bernoulli distribution** — a single yes/no outcome with some
  probability `p` of "yes." Every binary classifier (logistic regression,
  from Classical ML) is, at its core, estimating the parameter `p` of a
  Bernoulli distribution for each input.

## Conditional probability: the basis of Bayes' theorem

`P(A | B)` reads "the probability of A, given that B is true" — and it's
the building block of Naive Bayes (Classical ML course), which is built
directly from Bayes' theorem relating `P(class | features)` to
`P(features | class)`.

## In real code

<PyodidePlayground initialCode={`import numpy as np

rng = np.random.RandomState(0)
data = rng.normal(loc=50, scale=10, size=1000)  # mean=50, std=10

print("Mean:", round(np.mean(data), 2))
print("Variance:", round(np.var(data), 2))
print("Standard deviation:", round(np.std(data), 2))

# Standardizing (z-scoring): subtract the mean, divide by std --
# exactly the feature-scaling operation from the KNN lesson.
standardized = (data - np.mean(data)) / np.std(data)
print("Standardized mean (~0):", round(np.mean(standardized), 4))
print("Standardized std (~1):", round(np.std(standardized), 4))
`}
/>

Standardizing a feature so its mean is 0 and standard deviation is 1 is
exactly the transformation `StandardScaler` performs in scikit-learn — now
you've seen precisely what it computes under the hood.
